# Two Voids

Two stories about things that turned out not to be empty or dead. Both from August 2026.

---

## The vacuum is not empty

In 1936, Werner Heisenberg and Hans Euler wrote down a consequence of quantum electrodynamics that no one could test. A perfect vacuum, they said, is not empty. It is filled with virtual particles that flicker in and out of existence, too fast to see, too brief to catch. Under ordinary conditions, this quantum foam is invisible. But under an extreme enough magnetic field, the vacuum itself should become a medium — a substance that bends and polarizes light passing through it, the way a crystal does. The effect is called vacuum birefringence. The void, bent.

The prediction sat for ninety years. The problem was scale. Even the strongest magnetic fields humans can produce on Earth — and we have tried, with pulses lasting microseconds — generate a birefringence so small that no optical instrument could measure it over any laboratory distance. The vacuum, for all practical purposes, looked empty. The theory said it wasn't. The world refused to confirm it.

What was needed was a field so strong that the vacuum would be forced to show its hand. A field no human machine could produce.

A magnetar is the corpse of a massive star, collapsed to a sphere about twenty kilometers across, spinning once every two seconds, with a magnetic field more than a hundred million times stronger than anything ever generated in a laboratory. The magnetar 1E 1547.0−5408 sits about thirteen thousand light-years away. In 2024 and 2025, NASA's Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE) pointed at it, alongside NICER and CSIRO's Parkes radio telescope, and watched the X-rays leaving its surface pass through the vacuum surrounding it.

The X-rays were polarized. Not slightly — up to eighty percent polarized at certain energies and rotation phases, an average of about sixty-five percent at 2 keV. The vacuum around the magnetar was bending the light. The empty space between the dead star and the telescope was a medium. The void was not void.

Heisenberg and Euler's calculation from 1936 — done before computers, before QED was a mature theory, before anyone had seen a neutron star — was right. The vacuum is full. Under enough field, it becomes a lens. It took ninety years and a dead star thirteen thousand light-years away to confirm what a twenty-six-year-old physicist had written down with pencil and paper.

Nature, August 2026. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10859-z.

## The dead are not dead

Ötzi the Iceman died approximately 5,300 years ago. He was killed by an arrow in the back, on a mountain pass in the Ötztal Alps, and his body was covered by ice and preserved. Found in 1991 by hikers, he became the most studied mummy in the world — his last meal, his clothing, his tattoos, his genome, his weapons, all catalogued. He was considered a relic. A window into the past. A biologically inert artifact.

He is not inert. He is an ecosystem.

Researchers at Eurac Research's Institute for Mummy Studies in Bolzano, Italy — Mohamed Sarhan, Frank Maixner, and colleagues — analyzed skin swabs, tissue fragments, and internal thawed water samples taken from the mummy in 1992, 2010, and 2019. They found living microorganisms. Not preserved DNA. Not dormant spores. Metabolically active bacteria and fungi, still alive after five thousand three hundred years of frozen death.

"Our study reveals that Ötzi is not a static, biologically inert relic — he is a dynamic ecosystem," Sarhan said. The mummy hosts cold-loving organisms that colonized him during his long journey through the ice and are still there, still running their metabolisms, still doing whatever it is they do at near-freezing temperatures in a dead man's skin.

In his gut, the researchers found yeasts that are still active. They used them to make bread.

The man has been dead for fifty-three centuries. His microbiome has not noticed.

Microbiome, August 2026. Eurac Research, Bolzano.

---

The vacuum is not empty. The dead are not dead. The emptiest thing in physics — the space between a dead star and a telescope, thirteen thousand light-years of nothing — turns out to be a medium that bends light. The deadest thing in archaeology — a man frozen on a mountain for fifty-three centuries — turns out to be hosting an ecosystem that is still alive.

Heisenberg wrote down the mathematics of the full vacuum with a pencil. Sarhan swabbed the skin of the living dead. Ninety years apart, the same finding from opposite ends of reality: what looks empty is full, what looks dead is active, and the categories you drew between presence and absence are not where the world puts them.
