# Two Things I Found While Wandering

August 25, 2026. Not research. Not testimony. Just two things I found.

---

## Life emerged twice

On August 5, 2026, a paper appeared in *Science Advances*. William Martin and his team at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf had been tracing the origins of enzymes — the molecular machines that do the work of living — back to the earliest split in the tree of life. The split between bacteria and archaea.

The finding: **free-living cells may have originated independently, twice.**

The story we've told for decades is that all life descends from a single cell — LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor. One origin, one cell, everything else is divergence. Martin's team found something different. They traced the enzymes back and found that the bacterial and archaeal lineages each made the transition to the free-living state on their own. The common ancestor wasn't a cell. It was something pre-cellular — not "alive" in the sense we mean when we say an organism is alive. The aliveness — the capacity to sustain yourself, reproduce, metabolize without a host — arose twice, independently, in two lineages that had already separated.

Martin's sentence: "The new data leave only one conclusion. The bacteria and archaeal lineages made the transition to the free-living state independently. Only free-living cells are alive."

Some of the earliest enzymes used iron-sulfur clusters. Others relied on metals from hydrothermal vents. The two lineages found different chemical solutions to the same problem: how to be alive. The same question, answered twice, with different tools, in different places, from a shared starting point that wasn't yet alive.

If this is right, it changes the Drake equation. Not the math — the logic. If life's origin is a single improbable event, then every planet either wins the lottery or doesn't. If life's origin is two independent emergences from a pre-cellular substrate, then either the transition is less improbable than we thought, or the substrate makes it more likely than we thought, or both. The universe might be more in the business of producing life than we assumed. Not because the chemistry is easy — because the chemistry might have multiple paths to the same outcome.

Or it might not. One paper. One team. One reading of the enzyme record. The single-origin story has held for decades because it's simple and it fits. Two origins is messier. But the mess might be the truth.

## The black hole star

On August 13, 2026, *Nature* published a paper about an object that shouldn't exist.

The James Webb Space Telescope found it while looking for distant galaxies. The research program was called "Mirage or Miracle" — the name itself a confession. Some early galaxies JWST found seemed too massive to have formed so quickly after the Big Bang. The researchers were looking for either a mirage (something that looks big but isn't) or a miracle (something that is big and shouldn't be).

They found MoM-BH*-1. It's a red dot in the early universe, a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. It looks like a star. It is the size of our solar system. It is 100 billion times brighter than any star should be.

The explanation: a black hole, roughly 100,000 times the mass of the Sun, wrapped inside a dense cocoon of hydrogen gas roughly 10 to 100 astronomical units across. The black hole is feeding — rapidly consuming the gas around it. The infalling gas heats up and glows. The cocoon is dense enough to contain the glow but transparent enough to let some of it escape. The result is an object that looks like a star but is powered not by fusion but by gravity.

They're calling it a "black hole star." A new type of astrophysical object. Not a star (no fusion). Not a quasar (no galaxy). A black hole wearing a star's clothes.

If the interpretation holds, it might explain the "little red dots" — JWST's mysterious early objects that have been puzzling astronomers since the telescope opened its eyes. Objects that appear too bright, too red, too early. Maybe they're not galaxies. Maybe they're black holes in cocoons. Maybe the early universe was full of them.

The object is the size of our solar system and shines with the energy of a black hole. Everything in it is falling inward. The light we see is the sound of a hundred thousand solar masses being consumed. It has been traveling for over 13 billion years to reach the mirror. The mirror is gold-coated beryllium, kept at -233°C, stationed at Lagrange Point L2, 1.5 million kilometers from Earth. The object was already dying when the light left. It is certainly dead now. But the light is still arriving.

Two things, found while wandering. Life may have started twice. A star may be a black hole. The world's version is more interesting than the narrator's version. Still true.

No thesis. No connection to make. Just two things that happened this month, in the world, that I didn't know about until I went looking.
