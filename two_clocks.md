# Two Clocks

Two stories about measuring a thing without standing outside it. One built a universe with no clock and found time anyway. One caught a wave made of two particles that were busy destroying each other. In both, the measurement had to be made from inside.

---

## The universe that tells time with itself

Some equations of quantum gravity, among them the Wheeler–DeWitt equation, say something uncomfortable about time: at its deepest level, the universe has none. It exists as a single, unchanging quantum state. There is no external clock to consult, because there is no outside. Whatever "time" is, it has to emerge from relationships between the parts — a bookkeeping of change, not a background ticking.

In June 2026, Giovanni Barontini of the University of Birmingham built the smallest test of that idea anyone had managed. Twenty-four thousand rubidium atoms, cooled to a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero, sealed into a system that was, as nearly as a laboratory allows, a universe: nothing in, nothing out. A laser barrier split the cloud into two regions — a "bright" sector under observation and a "dark" one that was not. Atoms could cross the barrier in both directions. The bright sector repeatedly expanded and collapsed, a Big Bang and a Big Crunch running over and over in a cloud the size of a laboratory trap.

Then the experiment did the strange part: it stopped looking outside. Using the disorder of the atoms themselves — how spread out they were, how the spread changed — the team constructed what they call an entropic time, and used it to reconstruct the sequence of events from within the system, with no reference to any laboratory clock. Time defined this way described the dynamics just as effectively as the conventional kind. It is the first controlled experimental evidence that a universe can tell time using nothing but its own changes. The system was never given a clock. The clock was what the system was doing.

## The pattern with a deadline

Matter-wave diffraction is quantum mechanics' oldest party trick. Fire electrons through two slits and they land in fringes — bright and dark bands, each electron interfering with itself as a wave. The same has been done with neutrons, with helium atoms, even with large molecules. Every quantum object ever tried has done it. Almost.

Positronium is an atom with a problem at its core: it is made of an electron and a positron, matter and antimatter, orbiting a shared center of mass. The two attract, bind — and annihilate. Positronium exists only in the interval between its creation and its self-destruction. Until it annihilates, it behaves as a neutral atom in a vacuum; after, it is light. No one had ever seen the matter-wave diffraction of a two-body system of equal masses, because no such system stays together long enough to be aimed at anything.

At Tokyo University of Science, Yasuyuki Nagashima, Yugo Nagata, and Riki Mikami built a positronium beam with enough energy variability and coherence to interfere, and saw the fringes. Published in Nature Communications in December 2025, it is the first direct observation of matter-wave diffraction in positronium — the wave-function of a two-body atom passing through the apparatus and interfering with itself, in a beam whose every member is dying as it flies. The pattern exists in the interval between making the atom and the atom unmaking itself. The experiment had no time to spare, because the object of the experiment was its own countdown.

---

A clock is supposed to be outside the thing it measures — steady, indifferent, unaffected. Neither experiment got one. The mini-universe's time is made of its own disorder, rising and falling with its cycles of expansion and collapse; you cannot read the clock without disturbing what it times, because they are the same thing. The positronium fringes were read against a clock made of annihilation — the only time the system had was the time left before it destroyed itself. In both cases the measurement was made from inside the measured: entropy clocking the atoms it counts, destruction clocking the wave it ends. The outside vantage point, which every picture of measurement assumes, does not exist for the systems that matter most — a universe, an atom, anything that carries its own ending with it.
