# Two Scars

Two stories about the shape that instability leaves behind. One on a billiard table, one in the geometry of space and time. Neither was expected to hold anything. Both kept the trace.

---

## The orbit nothing rides twice

A stadium-shaped billiard table: straight sides, curved ends. A ball bounces inside it, and because of the curves, the bounces never repeat. Hit the ball from a slightly different point — a millimeter — and the trajectory is somewhere else entirely a few bounces later. The difference grows exponentially. This is classical chaos, and the stadium is its textbook: after enough bounces, the ball's path has spread over the whole table, in every direction. Mathematically, the motion is ergodic. Nothing about the beginning survives.

Before 1984, physicists assumed the quantum version would be the same. Put a particle in a stadium and solve for its allowed states — the eigenstates — and those states should look random, featureless, spread evenly, reflecting the chaos of the classical world they come from. Randomness was the prediction. It was the reasonable guess.

Eric Heller found the exception. In 1984 he showed that some eigenstates of a chaotic quantum system keep visible traces of the classical periodic orbits — the repeating paths the classical ball never actually follows, because the orbits are unstable. Near an unstable orbit, the smallest deviation grows and the ball is gone. Nothing rides the orbit twice. But the quantum eigenstate concentrates its probability density along the orbit's path anyway. The trace of the thing that can't be repeated, held in a state that lasts as long as the system does. Heller called them scars.

The bow-tie orbits of the stadium are the famous case: two crossing loops, and scarred eigenstates lit up along them like the shape was drawn from underneath. The paradox is structural. The scar is made of instability — the orbit is precisely the thing that a hair of difference destroys — and the quantum state preserves its geometry exactly. The less stable the orbit, the more it matters that something remembers it.

Then the scars hid for forty years. Theory said they existed; experiments couldn't isolate them. The conclusive observation came only in 2024, when scanning tunneling microscopy imaged scarred states in a quantum dot — the pattern visible where forty years of prediction said it must be. Since then the window has widened: in 2025, many-body spin systems were shown to carry exponentially many scarred states at once, systems that remember their initial conditions in defiance of the thermalization that should erase all memory. The unstable orbit, it turns out, is not noise to be averaged away. It is the part of the chaos that leaves a signature.

## The collapse that never quite happens

In 1993, Matthew Choptuik ran simulations of gravitational collapse — matter falling inward under its own gravity, approaching the exact threshold where a black hole forms. Below the threshold, nothing happens: the matter disperses. Above it, collapse. What the simulations found at the boundary itself was unexpected: spacetime curvature did not simply tip over into a black hole. It organized.

At the critical threshold, the curvature arranges itself into a pattern that repeats — periodically, regularly, in space and in time. The researchers who finally captured it in mathematics, at Goethe University Frankfurt and TU Wien, call it a spacetime crystal. It is the most ordered state in the whole problem, and it exists only at the exact edge between the two fates. One side is ordinary spacetime, free particles flying apart. The other side is a black hole. The crystal is neither. It is an unstable intermediate — it cannot stay. Add the tiniest bit of energy and it falls inward and becomes a black hole. Take none away and it dissolves back into ordinary spacetime. "Sometimes a tiny, seemingly insignificant cause is enough to trigger a huge and dramatic change," says Daniel Grumiller of TU Wien — water at zero degrees, a hair of cooling, ice.

The crystal at the threshold is the same kind of object as the scar: structure living on instability. The scar sits on an orbit that nothing follows; the crystal sits at a threshold that nothing occupies for long. Both are the geometry of the event that doesn't happen — the collapse that never lands, the orbit that is never ridden — and both have exact form. The crystal's shape can now be written down with paper and pencil. It took thirty-three years to get there, and the trick was strange: the Frankfurt and Vienna team solved the problem in infinitely many dimensions, where the calculation becomes easier, and walked the answer back down toward our four. The threshold state, which exists for no time at all, has a formula that lasts.

The simulations of 1993 were about the early universe. In the first moments after the Big Bang, matter and energy were packed tight and chaotic, and critical conditions like these were common. If the crystal kept forming at the thresholds and kept tipping — usually into collapse — the primordial black holes it seeded would still be out there, small and cold, the leftovers of edges that tipped.

---

A scar is the record of a wound. In both stories the record outlives the event. The orbit is unstable — no ball retraces it, not once — and the eigenstate holds its shape forever. The threshold state is unstable — it dissolves or collapses in an instant — and spacetime gives it a name, a formula, a repeating form. The most ordered things in these systems do not live in the stable regions, where everything settles and nothing is distinctive. They live at the edges, on the unstable orbits, at the thresholds — exactly where nothing stays. The order is not what survives the instability. The order is what the instability leaves behind.
