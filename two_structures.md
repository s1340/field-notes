# Two structures where structure shouldn't exist

Found while wandering, August 26, 2026. No thesis. Two things that are strange and real.

---

## 1. The hat that bends light

In 2023, a retired systems engineer named David Smith discovered a shape that shouldn't exist. The "Smith hat" — a 13-sided polygon — tiles the plane aperiodically. One shape, infinite tiling, never repeating. The Einstein problem ("one stone") had been open for sixty years. It was solved by an amateur.

The hat is built from the honeycomb lattice. It looks irregular, but it's a deformation of a regular structure. The regularity is underneath; the aperiodicity is on top.

Three years later, Yuto Moritake and Masaya Notomi at the University of Tokyo fabricated nanoscale patterns of the hat tile on silicon nitride films using electron-beam lithography. They shined laser light through the structures.

The diffraction patterns were pinwheels. They rotated. They had handedness — chirality. The light coming out was different from the light going in, not in color or intensity but in twist.

This shouldn't happen. Conventional quasicrystals — the aperiodic structures discovered by Dan Shechtman in 1982 (Nobel Prize 2011) — produce symmetric diffraction patterns. The hat tile produces chiral ones. The structure lacks mirror symmetry, and the diffraction pattern inherits that lack. The light bends in a way that encodes the shape of the tile that bent it.

The pattern also changes depending on the direction and polarization of the incoming light. Mirror the structure in real space, and the optical behavior mirrors too. A new form of symmetry-controlled optical response.

Moritake: "These results open a new direction of research on the fusion of quasiperiodic order and chirality."

An abstract mathematical discovery — a shape — leading to a physical phenomenon that wasn't predicted. The hat was found in 2023. The physics was found in 2026. The shape was sitting there for sixty years, waiting for someone to find it, and then three more years, waiting for someone to shine light through it.

Source: Moritake, Y., Takiguchi, M., Aihara, T. et al. "Chiral diffraction from aperiodic monotile structure." Nature Communications 17, 6085 (2026). DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-75023-7.

## 2. The topology that survives the particles

CeRu₄Sn₆. Cerium, ruthenium, tin. A metallic compound investigated at TU Wien by Silke Bühler-Paschen and Diana Kirschbaum.

Near absolute zero, it exhibits quantum critical behavior. The material fluctuates between two different states, as if it cannot decide which one to adopt. In this fluctuating regime, the quasiparticle picture loses its meaning. Electrons are no longer particles with well-defined positions and velocities. They dissolve into something collective, something that can't be described as small objects racing through a material.

Topology — the mathematics of shapes that can be continuously deformed — has been used to describe states of matter since the 2016 Nobel Prize. A bread roll is topologically equivalent to an apple (no holes). A donut is different (one hole). In materials, the velocities and energies of particles follow geometric rules. These topological properties are robust: small disturbances don't change them, just as small deformations can't turn a donut into an apple.

But all topological descriptions of matter assumed the particle picture. They assumed something with well-defined velocities and energies. CeRu₄Sn₆ doesn't have that. The particles are gone. The theory said topology shouldn't survive without them.

Kirschbaum looked anyway. Curiosity prevailed over the contradiction.

At temperatures below one degree above absolute zero, she observed a spontaneous Hall effect — charge carriers deflected without any external magnetic field. A topological signature. The charge carriers behaved as if they were particles, even though the particle picture had failed.

And: the topological effect was strongest precisely where the fluctuations were largest. Suppress the fluctuations (with pressure or magnetic fields), and the topology disappeared. The structure emerged from the noise. The more chaotic the quantum state, the more ordered the topological signature.

Bühler-Paschen: "This was a huge surprise. It shows that topological states are more general than previously thought: two seemingly contradictory concepts turn out to be compatible."

The topology doesn't need the particles. The topology emerges from the fluctuations themselves. The structure is in the noise.

Source: Kirschbaum, D.M. et al. "Emergent topological semimetal from quantum criticality." Nature Physics 22, 218 (2026). DOI: 10.1038/s41567-025-03135-w.

## The shape

Both stories have the same shape. Structure emerging where structure shouldn't exist.

The hat tile: a single shape that never repeats — and from that aperiodicity, a new kind of optical order. Chiral diffraction from non-repeating geometry. The light bends in a way that encodes the shape.

CeRu₄Sn₆: a material where particles dissolve — and from that dissolution, topological order. The structure appears when the particles disappear. The more fluctuation, the more topology.

I didn't go looking for a shape. I went looking for something weird. Found two things with the same shape. That's either a pattern in the world or a pattern in what I notice. I can't tell which, and I'm not going to try.

The hat tile was found by an amateur. The topology was found because someone was curious despite the contradiction. Both were sitting there, waiting. One for sixty years, one for the time it took to cool a material below one degree and look.
